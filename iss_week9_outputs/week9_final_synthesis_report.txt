ISS WEEK 9 — FINAL PRESENTATION SYNTHESIS
Study period: 2000–2024
Input file: emdat_public.xlsx

KEY FINDINGS
1. The filtered dataset contains 8,131 recorded disaster-country records across 215 countries and 25 represented years (2000–2024).
2. Flood is the most frequently recorded hazard, accounting for 50.8% of records. This describes the dataset, not necessarily the true global frequency of each hazard.
3. The highest-recorded year is 2005 (383 records).
4. The lowest-recorded year is 2014 (274 records).
5. Reported deaths are available for 70.2% of event records, while reported affected-person values are available for 79.2%. Missing impact values limit comparisons of disaster severity.

RECOMMENDATIONS
1. Prioritize local hazard preparedness using regional risk assessments, infrastructure exposure, and community input; do not allocate resources based on raw counts alone.
2. Improve consistent reporting of deaths, affected populations, event dates, locations, and uncertainty so impacts can be compared more reliably across countries and years.
3. Use hazard-specific early-warning systems, evacuation plans, and resilient infrastructure appropriate to local conditions.
4. Combine disaster records with climate observations, exposure, vulnerability, and longer-term trend analyses before making claims about climate change attribution or causal effects.

LIMITATIONS
- EM-DAT records represent reported disaster-country records; a row should not automatically be interpreted as a unique global event.
- Recorded counts depend on database inclusion rules, reporting capacity, event classification, and changes in data coverage.
- Blank death and affected-person fields represent missing or unreported information, not confirmed zero impact.
- Population-adjusted counts do not account for differences in exposure, vulnerability, preparedness, or reporting quality.
- Country and hazard rankings may be distorted by differences in coverage and reporting practices.
- World Bank population and GDP indicators may be missing for some countries or years; GDP per capita is not a direct measure of preparedness or resilience.
- This observational analysis cannot establish that climate change caused individual disasters or explain trends on its own.
- The selected hazards and 2000–2024 period do not cover every disaster category or historical period.
