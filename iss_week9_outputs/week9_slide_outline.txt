Slide 7 — Key Findings
- The filtered dataset contains 8,131 recorded disaster-country records across 215 countries and 25 represented years (2000–2024).
- Flood is the most frequently recorded hazard, accounting for 50.8% of records. This describes the dataset, not necessarily the true global frequency of each hazard.
- The highest-recorded year is 2005 (383 records).
- The lowest-recorded year is 2014 (274 records).
- Reported deaths are available for 70.2% of event records, while reported affected-person values are available for 79.2%. Missing impact values limit comparisons of disaster severity.

Slide 8 — Recommendations
- Prioritize local hazard preparedness using regional risk assessments, infrastructure exposure, and community input; do not allocate resources based on raw counts alone.
- Improve consistent reporting of deaths, affected populations, event dates, locations, and uncertainty so impacts can be compared more reliably across countries and years.
- Use hazard-specific early-warning systems, evacuation plans, and resilient infrastructure appropriate to local conditions.
- Combine disaster records with climate observations, exposure, vulnerability, and longer-term trend analyses before making claims about climate change attribution or causal effects.

Slide 9 — Limitations
- EM-DAT records represent reported disaster-country records; a row should not automatically be interpreted as a unique global event.
- Recorded counts depend on database inclusion rules, reporting capacity, event classification, and changes in data coverage.
- Blank death and affected-person fields represent missing or unreported information, not confirmed zero impact.
- Population-adjusted counts do not account for differences in exposure, vulnerability, preparedness, or reporting quality.
- Country and hazard rankings may be distorted by differences in coverage and reporting practices.
- World Bank population and GDP indicators may be missing for some countries or years; GDP per capita is not a direct measure of preparedness or resilience.
- This observational analysis cannot establish that climate change caused individual disasters or explain trends on its own.
- The selected hazards and 2000–2024 period do not cover every disaster category or historical period.
